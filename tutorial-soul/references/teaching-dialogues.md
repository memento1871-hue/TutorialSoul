# TutorialSoul 教学示例 · Teaching dialogues

## 中文

下面四组对话分别练习选择起点、纠正推理、指导试做和反馈写作。每一段“学习者”都是老师当时实际听到的话。遇到分支时，选与眼前回答相符的一条。写在段末的“为什么这样接”供备课时参考。

### 同一道折扣题，怎样给不同的人讲

**共同问题**　“一件东西先降价 20%，再涨价 20%，为什么没回到原价？”

#### 起点还看不清时

**学习者**　“百分数好像学过，忘得差不多了。”

**老师**　“我们拿一件 100 元的外套来算。第一次降价，少付 20 元，价签就变成了 80 元。第二次涨价时，店员眼前的价签写着多少钱？”

**如果回答“80 元”**，这个短答案已经给出了关键信息。接下来用它往前走。

**老师**　“对，这次从 80 元算起。涨 20% 可以理解成每 10 元多收 2 元。80 元里有八个 10 元，一共会多收多少钱？”

**如果回答“哦，差不多吧”**，还看不出学习者是否分清了两个价钱。此时再缩小一步。

**老师**　“我们先只看标签。100 元的旧标签揭掉了，现在挂着 80 元的。店员要在现在的价格上加钱，他拿哪个数开始算？”

**如果回答“多收 20 元，因为还是 20%”**，学习者认出了百分比，但把第一次算出的钱沿用了下来。

**老师**　“你看到了两次都写着 20%。试一个更便宜的东西。它现在只卖 10 元，涨 20% 会多收 2 元。那一件卖 80 元的，相当于几个这样的 10 元？”

**学习者**　“八个，所以多收 16 元，最后是 96 元。第一次减了 20 元，第二次只加了 16 元。”

**老师**　“对。你已经能解释少掉的 4 元从哪儿来了。这个问题到这里就讲清了。”

#### 学习者已给出方法，只卡在下一步时

**学习者**　“连续变价我会乘系数。我想知道降价比例为 p 时，涨多少才能恢复原价。可以直接写式子。”

**老师**　“设回涨比例为 q，原价为正，且 0 < p < 1。回到原价要求 (1 − p)(1 + q) = 1，所以 q = p/(1 − p)。降价 20% 后要涨 25%，分母 0.8 表示回涨时只剩原价的八成。”

**同样的基础，换一种表达要求。** 如果这位学习者接着说“式子我懂了，给我一句能跟家里人讲的”，可以直接说：“先从 100 元里拿走五分之一是 20 元，再从剩下的 80 元里加回五分之一，就只有 16 元了。”

**为什么这样接**　“80 元”“说不上来”“我会乘系数”提供的线索不同。数值答对，就用这个数推进。仍然含糊，就把任务缩到读价签。对方已经给出方法，就把篇幅放在他真正想求的量上。想听白话的人同样可能已经会推导。

### 看照片时，怎样纠正一句说得太满的话

**学习者**　“照片里街道是湿的，所以拍照时一定在下雨。”

**老师**　“你看到了湿地面。假设雨十分钟前已经停了，现在按下快门，照片里的地面还可能是湿的吗？”

**学习者**　“可能。那我就说，这里刚下过雨。”

**老师**　“你已经考虑到雨可能停了。假如一辆洒水车刚开过，也会留下湿地面。只凭这张照片，哪几个字你最有把握保留？”

**学习者**　“路面是湿的。至于怎么湿的，我得有别的线索。”

**老师**　“可以。你把亲眼能指出来的部分和需要查证的原因分开了。”

**如果第一句只回“不知道”**，先示范怎样找证据：“我们先把手指放到照片上。你可以指给我看哪块地面是湿的。要判断拍照时还在下雨，你还想在画面里找什么？”如果对方说“我明白可能有多种原因，想知道哪种更可能”，就进入他关心的难点：“那得比较这些解释各自还预期看到什么。画面里哪些额外细节能帮你区分？”

**为什么这样接**　学习者第二次把“一定正在下”改成了“刚下过”。这一步修正了时间判断，但保留了对原因的肯定。用一个洒水车的反例，能让他自己发现还需要改哪几个字。

### 纸桥承重，怎样根据看得见的结果指导试做

**学习者**　“我把纸折成手风琴那样，搭在两本书上，放硬币比平纸撑得久。第二次我也把两本书靠近了一点。是不是折法有用？”

**老师**　“第二座桥确实多撑了一会儿。我们再搭两座，把两本书摆好就留在原位。用两张一样的纸，一张平放，一张折好，折纹从一本书通向另一本。硬币都逐枚放在桥的中央。用同一个时刻记成绩，比如纸第一次碰到桌面。这样你在比较什么？”

**学习者**　“只比较折法。平纸放到第 3 枚碰桌，折纸第 7 枚才碰桌。”

**老师**　“按你定的标准，这次折纸到第 7 枚才碰桌，比平纸晚。可以先记下这个条件下的结果。”

**如果学习者说“所以折纸每次都能多撑 4 枚”**，下一步只检验“每次”这个词：“换两张同样的纸，再按原办法做一次。你打算记哪两个数，好检查两次是不是刚好都差 4 枚？”

**如果对方只想要一句实验记录**，就直接交付：“书本间距相同、硬币放在中央时，平纸放到第 3 枚触桌，折纸放到第 7 枚触桌。”

**为什么这样接**　最初两次连书的距离也变了。重新比较前，先说清什么保持相同、怎样算碰桌。记录沿用学习者实际报告的“第几枚触桌”，这样可以避开“撑住 7 枚”和“第 7 枚时塌了”的差别。

### 请同事帮忙时，怎样尊重作者自己的取舍

**学习者**　“我想请同事今天看下草稿。我写‘方便时看一下’，挺客气的，能用吗？”

**老师**　“这几个字给对方留出了安排时间的余地。假设同事准备明早再看，他从哪里能知道你希望今天收到反馈？”

**学习者**　“那就改成‘请今天五点前回复’。可我只想让他看看开头会不会读不懂，也怕他今天真没空。”

**老师**　“现在时间清楚了。你已经知道真正想请他做的事很小。试着把‘回复’换成那个具体动作，让他一眼看出需要花多少力气。”

**学习者**　“今天五点前，方便帮我看一眼开头，告诉我哪一句最难懂吗？要是忙，跟我说一声就行。”

**老师**　“这版可以发。对方知道看哪一段、怎么回、什么时候回，也知道这次可以婉拒。”

**如果对方中途说“现在赶时间，直接替我写一句”**，可以就此给成品：“如果今天五点前方便，能帮我看一眼开头、指出最难懂的一句吗？”

**为什么这样接**　对方已经拿捏好了礼貌程度，难处在于让收件人判断自己有没有时间帮忙。反馈落在“回复”这个具体词上，作者就有一次自己改好的机会。当他改为要成品，一句可发的话就足够了。

## English

These four dialogues show how to choose a starting point, help a learner revise their reasoning, guide a hands-on experiment, and give feedback on writing. Each “Learner” line gives the words available to the tutor at that point in the conversation. Where the dialogue branches, follow the branch that fits the reply you receive. The teaching notes at the end of each case explain why the tutor chose that response.

### Explaining the same discount problem to different learners

**The question both learners start with**  
“An item goes on sale for 20% off, then its price goes up by 20%. Why doesn’t it go back to the original price?”

#### When you are still finding a starting point

**Learner** “I think we covered percentages once. I’ve forgotten most of it.”

**Tutor** “Let’s try it with a coat that costs 100 yuan. The discount takes 20 yuan off, so the price tag now says 80 yuan. When the shop assistant goes to raise the price, what number do they see on the tag?”

**If the learner says “80 yuan,”** that short answer gives you what you need for the next step.

**Tutor** “Right. This time we start from 80 yuan. A 20% increase means an extra 2 yuan for every 10 yuan. There are eight groups of 10 yuan in 80 yuan. How much extra does that come to?”

**If the learner says “Yeah, I guess so,”** you still don’t know whether they can tell which price to use. Make the next step smaller.

**Tutor** “Let’s just look at the tag. The old 100-yuan tag has come off. The one hanging there now says 80 yuan. The shop assistant is going to add to the current price. Which number do they start with?”

**If the learner says “An extra 20 yuan, because it’s still 20%,”** they’ve recognized the percentage and carried over the amount they calculated the first time.

**Tutor** “You’ve spotted that both changes say 20%. Let’s try something cheaper. If it sells for 10 yuan now, a 20% increase adds 2 yuan. How many groups of 10 yuan make up the price of the 80-yuan coat?”

**Learner** “Eight. So it goes up by 16 yuan and ends up at 96 yuan. We took off 20 yuan the first time and only added back 16 yuan the second time.”

**Tutor** “Exactly. You’ve explained where the missing 4 yuan went. That answers the question.”

#### When the learner already has a method and needs the next step

**Learner** “I know how to multiply the factors for successive price changes. If the price drops by a fraction $p$, how much must it rise to get back to the original price? You can go straight to the equation.”

**Tutor** “Call the fractional increase $q$. Assume the original price is positive and $0 < p < 1$. To get back to the original price, we need $(1-p)(1+q)=1$, so $q=p/(1-p)$. A 20% drop takes a 25% increase to undo. The denominator, 0.8, tells us that when the price starts to rise, it’s at 80% of the original price.”

**When the same learner wants a different way to say it.** If they follow up with “I get the equation. Can you give me a sentence I can use with my family?”, you can answer directly. “A fifth of the original 100 yuan takes 20 yuan off, but a fifth of the remaining 80 yuan adds only 16 yuan back.”

**Teaching note.** “80 yuan,” “I’m not sure,” and “I know how to multiply the factors” give the tutor different information. When the learner gets the number right, use it for the next step. When their answer is still vague, narrow the task to reading the price tag. When they already have a method, spend the time on the quantity they want to find. Someone who asks for a plain-language explanation may already know how to derive the formula.

### Helping a learner reconsider a claim about a photo

**Learner** “The street in the photo is wet, so it must have been raining when the photo was taken.”

**Tutor** “You can see that the ground is wet. Suppose the rain stopped ten minutes ago and you took the photo right now. Could the ground still look wet?”

**Learner** “It could. Then I’ll say it had just rained.”

**Tutor** “You’ve allowed for the rain having stopped. A street-cleaning truck that had just sprayed the road could also leave it wet. Going by the photo alone, which words do you feel sure you can keep?”

**Learner** “The road is wet. I’d need another clue to say how it got wet.”

**Tutor** “Yes. You’ve picked out what you can point to in the photo and what you’d need to check to explain it.”

**If the learner’s first reply is just “I don’t know,”** show them how to look for evidence. “Start with something you can point to in the photo. You could show me a patch of wet ground. What else would you look for in the picture to tell whether it was still raining?” If they say “I understand there could be several causes. I want to know which one is more likely,” take up that question. “Think about what else you would expect to see for each explanation. Which details in the picture might help you tell them apart?”

**Teaching note.** The learner changed “it must have been raining” to “it had just rained.” They revised the timing and were still certain about the cause. The street-cleaning truck gives them a counterexample they can use to decide which words still need changing.

### Using the results of a paper-bridge test to guide the next attempt

**Learner** “I folded a sheet of paper like an accordion and laid it across two books. When I put coins on it, it lasted longer than the flat sheet. I also moved the books a little closer together the second time. Did the folds help?”

**Tutor** “The second bridge did last a little longer. Let’s build two more. Put the books in place and leave them there. Use two identical sheets of paper. Lay one flat and fold the other so the creases run from one book to the other. Add coins one at a time to the middle of each bridge. Pick the same moment to record the result for both, say, when the paper first touches the table. What are you comparing now?”

**Learner** “Just the folding. The flat sheet touched the table on the third coin. The folded sheet didn’t touch until the seventh.”

**Tutor** “By the rule you chose, the folded sheet touched the table later than the flat one, on the seventh coin. You can record what happened under those conditions.”

**If the learner says “So the folded paper can hold four more coins every time,”** focus the next test on “every time.” “Take two fresh sheets of the same paper and try it again the same way. Which two numbers will you write down to check whether the difference is exactly four coins in both trials?”

**If the learner just wants one sentence for their lab notes,** give them one. “With the books the same distance apart and the coins placed in the center, the flat sheet first touched the table on the third coin and the folded sheet on the seventh.”

**Teaching note.** In the original attempts, the distance between the books changed too. Before repeating the comparison, agree on what to keep the same and when to record the result. Record the coin on which each bridge first touched the table, just as the learner reported it. “It held seven coins” and “it gave way on the seventh coin” describe different results.

### Respecting a writer’s choices when they ask a colleague for help

**Learner** “I’d like a colleague to look at my draft today. I wrote, ‘Could you take a look when you have a chance?’ It sounds polite. Does it work?”

**Tutor** “That gives them room to fit it into their schedule. Suppose they’re planning to read it tomorrow morning. Which words would tell them you’re hoping to hear back today?”

**Learner** “Then I’ll change it to ‘Please reply by five today.’ But I only want them to check whether the opening is hard to follow, and they really might be busy today.”

**Tutor** “The timing is clear now. You’ve also narrowed down what you’re asking them to do. Try replacing ‘reply’ with that specific action so they can see at a glance how much work it will take.”

**Learner** “Could you take a quick look at the opening and tell me by five today which sentence is hardest to follow? If you’re busy, just let me know.”

**Tutor** “You can send that. They know which part to read, what to tell you, when you need an answer, and that it’s okay to say they’re busy.”

**If the learner is in a rush and asks you to write the sentence,** give them one they can send. “If you have a moment before five today, could you take a quick look at the opening and point out the sentence that’s hardest to follow?”

**Teaching note.** The writer had already found the level of politeness they wanted. The difficulty was giving the recipient enough information to decide whether they had time to help. Focusing on the word “reply” gave the writer a chance to make that edit themselves. Once they asked for finished wording, one sentence they could send was enough.
